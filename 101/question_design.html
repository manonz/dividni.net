Getting it right: Dividni question design - design considerations

Designing good questions for Dividni is a little different from how you may have approached single-use multiple-choice questions in the past.

Re-thinking your question design - assessing more than just knowledge

Many colleagues who first start out on Dividni have previously designed and used static multiple-choice questions for assessment. As such, they tend to focus on Truth questions that have one correct answer and a number of incorrect ones (distractors). In our experience, most colleagues tend to have used MCQ questions only to test recall.

While it is possible to use many of these in Dividni with only minor adaptation such as adding an extra distractor or two, this deprives you of many of the benefits of Dividni: A simple random rearrangement of the same set of answer options in each question instance using the same correct answer in each still leaves sideways glances as a workable exam strategy for students. It encourages memorisation of answers rather than understanding, and therefore "burns" questions after first use.

The aim of this page is to help you write questions you can re-use semester after semester (or year after year) and that do not really age.

Designing your questions the Dividni way means redesigning your questions so that the question stem allows for more than a single answer to be correct. For example: Instead of asking "What is 13+25?" and then having "38" as the single correct answer option, ask "Which of the following calculations is correct?" and use "13+25=38" and "27+12=39" etc. as correct answer options (in a Truth question). With a sufficiently large set of correct answer options, you now force your students to calculate rather than pull up last year's memorised answer. Mindlessly memorising meaningless facts becomes history.

This is but one example. Others:

  • Include a sufficiently complex graph or graphic in your question and offer various interpretations of it as "correct" and "incorrect" answer options. This forces students to analyse what they see.
  • In Medicine-related subjects, ask who you might give a particular medication to. As answer options, give short descriptions of potential patients (e.g., "female, 26, non-smoker, physically inactive, pregnant") to get students to analyse the appropriateness of prescribing.
  • In Chemistry, ask which reactions result in a particular chemical being produced. As answer options, give the incomplete reactions (minus the chemical mix at the end of the reaction). 
  • In a foreign language subject, present a partial sentence and correct and incorrect options that could possibly complete the sentence.

I'm sure you can think of a few examples in your field that will make students think and apply what they have learned.

Why five answer options?

A common query we get from new users of Dividni is why it uses 5 answer options and whether four are possible. These usually come from a desire of wanting to re-use old-style questions, but also sometimes from a desire to work with answer combinations such as "apples and oranges", "apples but not oranges", "oranges but not apples", and "neither apples nor oranges". There is almost always a way to rephrase these to add a 4th incorrect option.

Note also that one of the dangers of multiple-choice assessments is "pass by educated guessing", especially when the assessments are used in conjunction with take-home assessments vulnerable to unseen AI assistance or "helpers". Suppose a student is able to exclude two of four answer options and guesses between the remaining two options: This puts them within reach of a pass at the 50% mark. In questions with five answer options, means guessing between three remaining options and leaves them much further away from a pass.   

Pitfalls!

One thing to remember when using Dividni is that not all of a question's answer options or statements will be shown to every student - what they see depends on what Dividni randomly chooses for them and in which order it puts them. Do's and dont's:

  • Make each answer option / statement about equally difficult to accept and reject. Don't ask in one option whether "12 / 4 = 3" is correct and in the next whether "131 is the square root of 16171".
  • Let each option / statement stand on its own feet. Do not include references to other answer options like "the same as above, plus GST".
  • This includes not using options or statements like "all of the above" or "all of the others".
  • Asking which answer option comes closest to true or is best / largest / fastest / cheapest / lowest is generally asking for trouble. A good workaround here is to rank the various entities in each answer option. E.g., an answer option for the question Rank the fruit by vitamin C content from highest to lowest could read "orange, plum, kiwifruit, banana, apple" whereas another option might be "grapefruit, strawberry, pear, banana, apricot".
  • Avoid answer options or statements that give pointers to the veracity of others away. For example, having "10 + 12 = 22" in the "correct" answer pool and "10 +12 = 112" in the "incorrect" answer pool is bad question design, likewise statements such as "Auckland is on the NZ North Island" and "Auckland is on the NZ South Island", which also can't both be true.
  • Truth questions with only one true answer option are generally quite weak and age quickly. Avoid asking questions like "Who developed the general theory of relativity?" or "What does UNESCO stand for?" A better approach is to turn this into an XYZ question and ask "Which statements are true?", and make "Albert Einstein developed the general theory of relativity" a "correct" option and perhaps "UNESCO stands for United Nations Emergency Security Council" as an "incorrect" option.
    • In this context, avoid having statements like "Albert Einstein developed the general theory of relativity" a "correct" option together with "Marie Curie developed the general theory of relativity" as an "incorrect" option: The veracity of one gives away the incorrectness of the other, and not all students will see both statements. A question like this would be somewhat unfair if only some of the students who got to see the Albert Einstein statement also saw the Marie Curie one, whereas the rest would see "Richard Feynman invented the infrared telescope" instead.
  • Try and keep the length of each option / statement roughly the same - if some are just a couple of words and others are entire paragraphs, students who get a combination of the latter are disadvantaged compared to the former.
